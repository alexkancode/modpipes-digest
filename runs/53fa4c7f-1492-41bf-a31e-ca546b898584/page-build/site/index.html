Production brief

Genre: 90s trip hop crossed with fado

  • Tempo: verses in 6/8 at a dotted quarter of 60; choruses in 4/4 at a quarter of 90. The eighth note never changes (180 per minute), so the switch is a regrouping, felt in the drums, not a speed change. About 67 seconds.
  • Key: D minor (fado menor). Harmonic-minor C# on every A7. One B natural (G/B) per verse is the only lift.
  • Form: Verse 1 (8 bars) – Chorus (6) – Verse 2 (8) – Final chorus (7, extended on a held note). No intro beyond one bar of the loop; no outro beyond the loop fading on bass and vinyl noise.
  • Arrangement:
  • Verses: a brushed, swung 6/8 drum loop sampled from a worn record, quiet kick, dusty snare on the second pulse. Sub bass plays the falling chromatic line D, C#, C, B, Bb, A, one note per bar, and is the loudest thing after the voice. Soft Rhodes holds the chords. Vinyl crackle throughout.
  • Choruses: the drums flip to a flat, heavy 4/4 hip-hop beat, slow boom-bap, kick on 1, snare on 2 and 4, like the ward clock ticking. The Portuguese guitar answers every title line in the gap that follows it: two beats in bars 10 and 14, three eighths in bar 24, before the pickup "sem". It plays the hook cell itself back, A-A-D-C-A-G, in tremolo, a little behind the beat, like a second voice that knows the tune.
  • Bar 22: drums cut out. Only the guitar answers "e não sabes a letra".
  • Final chorus: a low string pad enters for the first time. On the held "até" (bars 27-28) everything thins to sub bass, the falling bass under it and the voice. A single bright tram bell rings once on beat 1 of bar 27, right after the voice sings "toca o sino do elétrico". The voice keeps singing over its decay. The loop then runs down to bass and crackle and stops.
  • Instrumentation: male countertenor; Portuguese guitar (twelve steel strings, bright, tremolo and arpeggio); sub bass; Rhodes; sampled breakbeat; vinyl noise; string pad (final chorus only); one tram bell.
  • Vocal direction: male countertenor in head voice, fragile and close to the microphone, breath audible, almost no vibrato in the verses. Sing to the nurse as "tu", softly, as if beside the bed. The first chorus is his request. Take an audible breath in the guitar gap before bar 23, then lean on the "-s" of "Cantas": she is singing now, without the words, so sing it a shade plainer, almost unsure of the tune. In the verses, lean on the stressed syllables on beats 1 and 4 and swallow the final unstressed vowels, as European Portuguese does. In the chorus, sing straighter and higher. Hold "até" on the high F for its full length with a slow, thin vibrato that only opens at the end; let it nearly break. Little pitch correction, short plate reverb.
  • Mood: three to five in the morning on a hospital ward; tired, tender, resigned. A song that tries to stretch the night instead of ending it.
  • Reference points in plain words: slow 90s Bristol-style trip hop with a lament bass and warped-vinyl drums; classic Lisbon fado singing with the guitar talking back to the voice; one late, held vocal climax instead of a big build.

About
A nurse working the night shift sits with a dying patient, wearing his mother's sweater, and sings a fado to him until five even though she doesn't know the words.
Hook line

Reader notes
The repeated "Canta até às cinco" is short, rhythmic and easy to sing back, and the image of singing without knowing the words is moving. The sal/saudade/mother imagery and the "vinte e oito" and the tram bell are cryptic, so the story is hard to follow on a single listen.
Match reason
The listener's reading keeps the core elements: a night-shift nurse, a patient she cannot save, and his request that she sing his mother's song, with the hours-to-dawn tension rendered as singing until five. It adds details not in the theme (the sweater, not knowing the words) and omits Lisbon, though fado implies it.
